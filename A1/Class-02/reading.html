Part 5 (Questions 31–36 • 2 puntos c/u)

Multiple Choice: The Ephemeral Nature of Digital Memory

We inhabit an age of unprecedented documentary abundance. Every minute, humanity generates petabytes of high-resolution photography, personal correspondence, scientific datasets, and civic records. Intuitively, one might assume that future historians will enjoy an omniscient window into the twenty-first century. Yet archivists and computer scientists increasingly warn of an unsettling paradox: our civilization may inadvertently be engineering a "digital dark age," leaving behind fewer recoverable traces than ancient Mesopotamia.

The fragility of digital artifacts stems from a dual vulnerability—physical degradation of storage media and rapid software obsolescence. Whereas inscriptions chiseled into basalt or penned onto acid-free rag paper remain legible to the naked eye across millennia, magnetic tapes demagnetize within decades and solid-state drives silently suffer from "bit rot" when unpowered. Even when raw binary sequences survive intact, interpreting them requires a complex ecosystem of proprietary operating systems, file formats, and rendering engines that routinely vanish within a single human generation.

Consider the fate of early multimedia encyclopedias or pioneering interactive web art from the 1990s. Many of these works are now functionally inaccessible, entombed inside obsolete optical discs or reliant on deprecated browser plugins. Attempting to resurrect them often resembles digital archaeology—requiring emulators painstakingly coded by hobbyists to mimic hardware architectures long abandoned by their manufacturers. Without continuous, resource-intensive migration from one format to the next, digital memory decays at an exponential rate.

Compounding this technical precariousness is the quiet privatization of our collective archive. For centuries, national libraries and municipal repositories operated under statutory mandates to preserve cultural heritage in perpetuity, insulated from commercial whims. Today, by contrast, the vast majority of public discourse, journalism, and visual culture resides on servers controlled by a handful of multinational technology corporations. Should a platform pivot its business model, succumb to insolvency, or simply deem legacy servers unprofitable, entire communities of cultural output can be erased overnight with the stroke of an algorithm.

Some technophiles place their faith in automated cloud redundancy and decentralized ledger technologies, arguing that distributing copies across global nodes guarantees immortality. However, replication alone does not solve the problem of semantic drift or link rot. Hyperlinks cited in peer-reviewed academic journals and supreme court rulings routinely decay within years, pointing either to vacant error pages or domains hijacked by unrelated commercial entities. A mirror of a broken reference merely multiplies the emptiness.

Preserving the twenty-first century will therefore demand more than superior silicon; it requires a profound cultural and institutional recalibration. We must abandon the comforting illusion that information stored in "the cloud" is effortlessly permanent. Curating digital heritage entails deliberate triage—deciding what merits conservation, funding open-source emulation frameworks, and reasserting public stewardship over records of enduring civic value before the present slips silently into oblivion.

Cross-Text Multiple Matching: Critics on Contemporary Brutalist Architecture

Lee los cuatro extractos críticos (A–D) y responde a las preguntas comparativas.

Critic A — Julian Vance

Post-war Brutalism emerged from an admirable egalitarian ethos, utilizing unadorned béton brut to construct civic libraries, universities, and social housing with sculptural honesty. Today, however, knee-jerk nostalgia blinds enthusiasts to the lived reality of these monoliths. Raw concrete weathers poorly in damp climates, streaking into oppressive, fortress-like facades that alienate pedestrians. While wholesale demolition carries an undeniable embodied-carbon penalty, reflexively slapping heritage protection onto every dilapidated concrete megastructure prevents cities from evolving toward warmer, human-scaled streetscapes.

Critic B — Elena Rostova

The current vogue for razing mid-century Brutalist landmarks in favor of generic glass-and-steel towers represents both an aesthetic impoverishment and an ecological scandal. Demolishing thousands of tons of structurally sound reinforced concrete releases staggering quantities of carbon that take decades of operational efficiency to offset. Moreover, the accusation that raw concrete inherently depresses the public stems largely from decades of deliberate municipal under-maintenance. Properly cleaned, planted with cascading greenery, and retrofitted internally, Brutalist structures possess a monumental dignity unmatched by contemporary commercial architecture.

Critic C — Marcus Sterling

Architectural historians rightly celebrate the utopian ambition behind twentieth-century Brutalism, yet we must acknowledge why these buildings provoke such visceral public hostility. Even when impeccably maintained, vast expanses of windowless, board-marked concrete project an intimidating authoritarianism that feels fundamentally hostile to everyday street life. That said, tearing them down is a reckless indulgence our climate cannot afford. Creative adaptive reuse—carving out light wells, softening plazas, and recladding thermal envelopes—offers a sensible middle path between preservationist dogma and ecological vandalism.

Critic D — Naomi Takahashi

Much of the renewed fascination with Brutalism on social media fetishizes dramatic chiaroscuro photography while ignoring profound structural and social flaws. Many post-war concrete complexes were conceived with scant regard for thermal insulation or acoustic comfort, rendering them prohibitively expensive to heat and miserable to inhabit. Unlike defenders who blame superficial neglect for their unpopularity, I contend that the aesthetic itself—austere, uncompromising, and inward-looking—was flawed from inception. Where retrofitting proves financially unviable, targeted replacement with low-carbon timber architecture is entirely justified.

Multiple Matching: The Ethics of Solar Geoengineering

Lee los cuatro perfiles científicos (A–D) y asocia cada afirmación (47–56) con el especialista correspondiente.

A — Dr. Aris Thorne (Atmospheric Physicist)

Stratospheric aerosol injection—mimicking large volcanic eruptions by dispersing reflective sulfate particles into the upper atmosphere—could theoretically lower global mean temperatures within months. From a purely radiative standpoint, the physics is robust and remarkably inexpensive compared to industrial decarbonization. Yet treating the atmosphere as a planetary thermostat introduces terrifying nonlinearities. Computer models consistently warn that altering solar insolation could disrupt regional monsoon patterns across South Asia, inadvertently triggering droughts in agricultural breadbaskets while cooling elsewhere. Worse still, if aerosol deployment were ever abruptly halted without deep emissions cuts, "termination shock" would unleash catastrophic, hyper-accelerated warming.

B — Prof. Mei-Ling Zhou (Climate Governance Ethicist)

My primary apprehension regarding solar radiation management is not technical malfunction, but political hubris. The mere prospect of a seemingly quick technological fix creates an acute "moral hazard," handing fossil-fuel incumbents and hesitant governments a convenient excuse to delay painful structural transitions away from hydrocarbons. Furthermore, who holds the hand on the global thermostat? Because stratospheric interventions affect every nation simultaneously, unilateral deployment by a single powerful state—or even a wealthy private consortium—would represent an unprecedented breach of international sovereignty, sparking geopolitical friction over every subsequent flood or crop failure.

C — Dr. Henrik Lindqvist (Marine Biogeochemist)

Reflecting sunlight back into space addresses only the thermal symptom of climate change while leaving its root chemical driver—excess atmospheric carbon dioxide—completely untouched. Even if solar geoengineering successfully stabilized surface temperatures, the world's oceans would continue absorbing millions of tons of CO₂ daily, relentlessly lowering seawater pH. Coral reefs, pteropods, and commercial shellfish fisheries would still face progressive dissolution from ocean acidification. Presenting solar reflection as a comprehensive climate cure therefore betrays a profound misunderstanding of Earth-system chemistry; at best, it could only ever serve as a temporary, strictly bounded supplement to rapid carbon removal.

D — Dr. Amara Okafor (Development Economist)

While affluent nations debate geoengineering in abstract ethical seminars, communities across the Global South are already enduring lethal wet-bulb temperatures and collapsing harvests. Refusing even to fund transparent, small-scale outdoor research into solar reflection strikes me as a luxury only temperate, wealthy countries can afford. If rapid warming pushes vulnerable regions past physiological survival thresholds before net-zero targets materialize, dismissing a potentially life-saving emergency brake out of ideological purity would be ethically indefensible. What we urgently need is inclusive governance led by climate-vulnerable nations themselves, ensuring research remains publicly accountable rather than banned outright.
