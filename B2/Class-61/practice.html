What Kind of Problem Solver Are You?

Different people solve their problems in different ways. Explore the three main approaches: Assertive, Meditative, and Cooperative.

Assertive

Action-Oriented Solver

Assertive people prefer action to talk. When they are faced with a problem, they immediately try to work out a solution and tackle it head-on.

"I don't waste time worrying. If my car breaks down, I immediately call the tow truck and figure out a backup route."

Meditative

Thoughtful & Research-Oriented

When meditative people have a problem, they sit and think about it, and might even do research. Sometimes, the answer comes if they don't act right away.

"I'd say I'm a meditative person. Before I bought my first car, I spent hours doing research on it by myself. It took me forever to decide!"

Cooperative

Collaborative & People-Oriented

Cooperative people think the easiest way to solve a problem is to ask for help. Another person's perspective can help cooperative people come up with solutions.

"Whenever I run into trouble at work, I gather my team or call a close friend to brainstorm ideas together."

How lighting & focus alter perception

Reading Article (T-77)

Painting and Problem Solving: FOUR LESSONS

By Barbara T. Armstrong

Have you ever spent hours trying to solve a personal or professional problem, only to end up feeling stuck? Sometimes, looking at how artists create paintings can offer surprising insights into solving everyday challenges. Here are four key lessons from the canvas:

Lesson 1: Shift Your Perspective and Focus

When an artist paints a landscape, changing the lighting or focus completely alters the artwork's mood. Similarly, if a problem seems impossible, try shifting your focus. Look at what you normally ignore—the background details rather than the main conflict.

Lesson 2: Step Back to See the Big Picture

Painters constantly step back from their canvas to evaluate the overall composition. When we are too close to a problem, small details overwhelm us. Stepping back allows us to see how all the pieces connect.

Lesson 3: Experiment with Different Tools and Approaches

If a paintbrush isn't working, an artist might switch to a palette knife or introduce new color contrast. If your usual problem-solving routine isn't yielding results, break your pattern and try an unconventional method.

Lesson 4: Don't Fear Starting Over or Making Mistakes

Famous master painters frequently painted over old canvases when they made mistakes. Mistakes are merely necessary layers of paint that add depth to the final masterpiece.

Giving Advice (Target Expressions):
  • "Here's an idea..." + (Imperative / Clause)
  • "If I were you, I'd..." + (Base Verb)
  • "Have you thought about..." + (-ing verb)?
  • "Why don't you..." + (Base Verb)?
Responding to Advice:
  • "I guess I could try that." (Hesitant agreement)
  • "I just might try that." (Enthusiastic agreement)
  • "That's a great idea!" (Positive response)
  • "I'm not so sure about that." (Polite doubt)
