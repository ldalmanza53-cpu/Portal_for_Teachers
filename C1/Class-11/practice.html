Unit 14 Discussion

Faces Matter: Implicit Facial Bias

Explore Psychologist Leslie Zebrowitz's research on how facial features (Baby-faced vs. Mature-faced) implicitly influence hiring recommendations and leadership assumptions.

Psychological Study Overview

Do face shapes decide job roles?

Psychologist Leslie Zebrowitz attached identical résumés of equally qualified people to photos of candidates with either baby faces (large eyes, round faces, soft features) or mature faces (angular jaw, smaller eyes, intense expression).

The Result: Business students recommended baby-faced individuals for jobs requiring compassionate/submissive traits (e.g. pediatric care), while mature-faced people were chosen for high-powered, intense roles like corporate litigation.

"People seemed to be chosen for jobs, or to select themselves into jobs, to match their appearance."

— Dr. Leslie Zebrowitz

Unit 15 Reading & Roleplay

Overcoming a Bad First Impression

Read Susan Fee's workplace advice and apply the 3-step sincere apology framework in real-time workplace crisis simulations.

Textbook Article: Susan Fee's Advice Breakdown

Click on highlighted words to reveal B1 definitions.

Have any of these situations happened to you? Forgetting someone’s name after you’ve just met, spilling coffee on your potential boss during an interview, or unintentionally insulting a co-worker on your first day? Ouch! You never have a second chance to make a first impression, so what happens when that impression is negative?

Below are Susan Fee's 6 essential rules to recover from a bad first impression:

As soon as you realize you may have offended someone, address it. The more time that passes, the more the story can become blown out of proportion. Take control of the situation by making your last impression a positive one.

Saying you're sorry is important, but overdoing it creates another uncomfortable situation. Your goal is to be responsible and sensitive. If you repeatedly bring up the past, groveling and begging for forgiveness, you defeat your purpose.

It's easy to assume that others think the worst of you, but usually what we imagine is far worse than reality. Don't start with "You must think I'm a total idiot." Instead say: "I'm uncomfortable with how I behaved yesterday..."

A sincere apology requires three clear steps:

  1. Don't blame external factors or other people.
  2. Acknowledge how your actions affected the other person (listen without defending yourself).
  3. Explain what you will do differently in the future to avoid repeating the mistake.

A little self-deprecating humor can save you, but make sure it is directed ONLY at yourself and does not increase anyone else's discomfort.

Communication has a cumulative effect. Every impression builds on previous ones. Overcoming a bad first impression requires all future behavior to be consistent with how you want to be perceived.
