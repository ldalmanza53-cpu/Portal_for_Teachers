Urban Park Rangers

Read the official article published in The New York Times about NYC summer park rangers. Learn about their daily duties, wildlife encounters, and urban nature challenges!

New York Times Feature

A SUMMER JOB that's a walk in the park

By Cara Buckley • Adapated for B1 Intermediate English

[1] Every summer, curious creatures infiltrate New York City's biggest parks. They number in the dozens, walk on their hind legs, are khaki in color, and exceedingly amiable by nature. Their most distinguished markings are their wide-brimmed Smokey Bear hats.

[2] They are Urban Park Ranger fellows, possessors of what may be the best, if not the most unusual, summer jobs in New York. "It's pretty laid-back – kind of therapeutic," said Mohammed Alomeri, 22, who works as a summer ranger at Fort Greene Park. "Every day, literally, is a walk in the park."

[3] The summer fellows supplement the corps of year-round Urban Park Rangers – who also wear Smokey Bear hats, but carry nightsticks and can issue summonses – during the parks' busiest season.

[4] Daily tasks can include giving nature walks, history talks, and children's craft classes; guiding lost visitors; asking people to leash their dogs; moving barbecue grills away from trees; explaining why feeding chicken nuggets to birds is unhealthy; and posing for tourists' cameras.

[5] Rangers field hundreds of calls from concerned park visitors when animals behave naturally. They get reports from people unnerved by raccoons, or mistakenly assuming sunning pigeons have broken wings, or growing vexed whenever animals wander onto jogging paths. Hawks hunting smaller birds will invariably yield dozens of calls.

[6] "They call and say, 'You gotta get that pigeon out,'" said Richard Simon, citywide ranger captain. "That's when we explain, 'This is the food chain.'"

[7] The rangers also handle animals truly in danger. They are handed injured baby birds, alerted to exotic animals dumped by owners, or led to stunned squirrels fallen from trees. "They usually land on their feet, but sometimes not so much," said year-round ranger Kathy Vasquez.
